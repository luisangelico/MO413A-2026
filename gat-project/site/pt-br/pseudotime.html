Pseudotime — how the model's focus shifts from healthy skin to metastasis

Run: run_multiseed_seed1

In one sentence. We sort every sample on a "tumor-likeness" axis and watch which genes the model relies on at each step. Genes whose importance rises as samples become more tumor-like are candidate signals of melanoma progression — as seen by the model.

What is an "embedding"?

Inside the trained network, each sample is summarized as a list of ~64 numbers — its embedding. Think of it as the model's fingerprint of the sample: similar embeddings = samples the model thinks look alike. We never look at this fingerprint directly during prediction; the network just uses it to decide a class. But we can look at it, and that's where this analysis starts.

What is "pseudotime" here?

We computed two anchors in embedding space: the average embedding of the Normal-skin samples (call it the "normal anchor") and the average embedding of the Metastasis samples (the "metastasis anchor"). Then we drew an imaginary line between the two and projected every sample onto that line. Each sample gets a number between 0 and 1:

  • 0 = embedding sits at the normal-skin anchor
  • 1 = embedding sits at the metastasis anchor
  • values in between = "somewhere along the way"

We split this 0→1 axis into 10 equal-width bins (deciles), and for each bin we average the model's attention scores across all samples that fell in that bin. The result is a picture of which genes the model focuses on at each step along the Normal → Metastasis axis.

Honest caveat. TCGA samples are not a time course — there is no patient followed across years. "Pseudotime" here is a cohort ordering built from how the trained model sees the data. It describes the model's view, not the biology of any single tumor evolving. Genes whose attention rises along this axis are hypotheses for progression markers, not confirmed ones.

Heatmap: gene attention across pseudotime

Rows = genes whose attention varies most across the axis. Columns = the 10 pseudotime deciles, ordered Normal-like → Metastasis-like. Each row is normalized by its own peak (so bright = "this gene's attention peaks here"). Rows are reordered so genes that peak early appear at the top, genes that peak late at the bottom.

Reading the figure: a row that's bright on the left and dark on the right means "the model paid attention to this gene mostly when looking at normal-like samples, and stopped caring as samples became more tumor-like." The reverse pattern points the other way.

Why does this matter?

This is the project's most ambitious figure: it ties together the embedding (the model's internal summary) and the attention (which genes the model uses) into one progression story. If keratin and skin-normal genes dominate the early deciles and fade in the late deciles, while melanocyte-lineage and melanoma- progression genes do the opposite, the model has internalized a coherent picture of how skin transitions toward melanoma — purely from expression and PPI topology, with no progression labels in training.
